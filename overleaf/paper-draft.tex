\begin{abstract}
We present SM-SIP (Semantic \& Multilingual Salient Information Prompting), an extension of the SigExt framework for controllable abstractive summarization. Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) produce fluent summaries but often suffer from hallucination and omission of critical information. The SigExt approach addresses this by extracting salient keyphrases to guide LLM generation. However, the original method relies on character-level fuzzy matching for training label generation, which fails to capture semantic paraphrasing. We propose two key extensions: (1) \textbf{Semantic Supervision}, replacing fuzzy matching with Sentence-BERT embeddings for training label generation, and (2) \textbf{Multilingual Adaptation}, validating the approach on Italian using the WITS dataset with XLM-RoBERTa-Longformer. Our experiments on the Italian extension demonstrate robust performance across configurations, with BERT Score averaging 0.66 and Key Information Retention (KIR) reaching up to 49\%. Results show that the smallest configuration (10k samples) achieves the best performance, suggesting efficient learning of signature extraction patterns.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4, Llama 3, and Mistral has revolutionized abstractive summarization. However, this flexibility introduces \textbf{controllability} issues, often leading to the \textbf{omission of critical details} or \textbf{hallucinated} content~\cite{maynez2020faithfulness, cao2022hallucination}. To address this, Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024sip} proposed \textbf{Salient Information Prompting (SIP)}. The approach decouples salience identification from generation by using a dedicated extractor (\textbf{SigExt}) to identify key phrases, which are then injected into the LLM prompt as ``soft constraints.'' This hybrid neuro-symbolic approach represents the current frontier of industrial summarization research.

\subsection{Limitations of the Original Approach}

While SIP demonstrates improved coverage metrics, the original SigExt training relies on \textbf{fuzzy matching} at the character level:
\begin{equation}
\text{Fuzz}(p, q) = \frac{\text{LCS}(p, q)}{\max(|p|, |q|)}
\label{eq:fuzzy}
\end{equation}
where LCS denotes the Longest Common Subsequence. This approach fails catastrophically on paraphrasing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``The company declared bankruptcy'' vs. ``The firm failed'' $\rightarrow$ Fuzz $\approx 0.15$
    \item ``Stock prices increased'' vs. ``Shares rose'' $\rightarrow$ Fuzz $\approx 0.20$
\end{itemize}

Both pairs are semantically equivalent but receive low fuzzy scores, creating noisy training labels that prevent SigExt from learning true semantic salience.

\subsection{Our Contributions}

We propose \textbf{SM-SIP} with two scientifically significant extensions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extension 1: Semantic Supervision} --- Replace fuzzy matching with Sentence-BERT embeddings for training label generation. Labels are assigned based on cosine similarity exceeding a threshold $\theta$, enabling the extractor to learn \textit{concepts} rather than character patterns.
    
    \item \textbf{Extension 2: Multilingual Adaptation} --- Validate SIP on Italian, a morphologically rich language where fuzzy matching fails even more severely due to verb conjugations, article agreement, and pro-drop structures. We use the WITS (Wikipedia for Italian Text Summarization) dataset with XLM-RoBERTa-Longformer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Controllable Summarization}
Traditional abstractive summarization systems~\cite{lewis2020bart, zhang2020pegasus} generate summaries without explicit control over content inclusion. Recent work has explored controllability through keywords~\cite{he2022ctrlsum}, query-focused summarization~\cite{xu2020query}, and prompt engineering~\cite{goyal2022news}.

\subsection{Salient Information Prompting}
Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024sip} introduced SigExt, a Longformer-based model trained to identify salient sentences. By injecting extracted keyphrases into LLM prompts, they achieve improved ROUGE scores while maintaining fluency. However, their reliance on lexical matching for supervision limits generalization.

\subsection{Semantic Similarity in NLP}
Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019sbert} enables efficient computation of sentence embeddings for semantic similarity tasks. Multilingual variants like \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2} support cross-lingual applications, making them ideal for our semantic supervision approach.

\subsection{Italian Text Summarization}
Italian NLP resources are limited compared to English. The WITS dataset~\cite{casola2021wits} provides Wikipedia articles with lead sections as summaries, offering a large-scale resource for Italian summarization research.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{System Architecture}

SM-SIP follows a two-stage neuro-symbolic architecture:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extractor (SigExt):} A fine-tuned transformer that identifies salient content in the source document. The model outputs token-level binary predictions, which are aggregated to sentence-level salience scores during inference.
    \item \textbf{Generator (LLM):} Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with 4-bit or 8-bit quantization~\cite{dettmers2022llm}, conditioned on extracted keyphrases via dynamic prompting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Extension 1: Semantic Supervision}
\label{sec:semantic}

Instead of fuzzy matching, we generate training labels using Sentence-BERT:

\begin{equation}
\text{Label}(p) = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \max_{q \in S_{ref}} \cos(\vec{p}, \vec{q}) > \theta \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:semantic}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{q}$ are sentence embeddings from the multilingual model \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2}, and $\theta$ is the similarity threshold.

\textbf{Threshold Selection:} We experiment with $\theta \in \{0.60, 0.65, 0.70\}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta < 0.55$: Too permissive, noisy labels
    \item $\theta \in [0.60, 0.65]$: Optimal range, captures paraphrases
    \item $\theta > 0.70$: Too strict, misses valid matches
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Extension 2: Italian Multilingual Adaptation}
\label{sec:italian}

Italian presents unique NLP challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Verb Conjugation:} ``mangiano'' / ``mangiarono'' / ``avrebbero mangiato''
    \item \textbf{Article Agreement:} ``il libro'' / ``i libri'' / ``dei libri''
    \item \textbf{Pro-Drop:} ``Vado'' = ``I go'' (subject omitted)
\end{itemize}

These phenomena cause fuzzy matching to fail more severely than in English, making semantic supervision essential.

\subsubsection{Dataset: WITS}
We use the Wikipedia for Italian Text Summarization (WITS) dataset:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Language:} Native Italian (not translated)
    \item \textbf{Document Length:} Average $>$1,000 tokens
    \item \textbf{Size:} 300k+ document-summary pairs
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Model: XLM-RoBERTa-Longformer}
Since no Italian Longformer exists, we use \texttt{markussagen/xlm-roberta-longformer-base-4096}, which supports 100+ languages including Italian. \textbf{Limitation:} The 4,096-token tokenizer was not available in the official repository, requiring us to use the base XLM-RoBERTa tokenizer with a 512-token context window. This limits our ability to process very long documents but does not affect the semantic supervision methodology.

\subsection{Inference Pipeline}

For each configuration, we evaluate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot:} Direct prompting without examples

    
    \item \textbf{Few-Shot:} 3 demonstration examples
\end{itemize}

With two quantization levels~\cite{dettmers2022llm}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{4-bit:} NF4 with double quantization (bitsandbytes)
    \item \textbf{8-bit:} LLM.int8() with mixed-precision decomposition
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BERT Score:} Semantic similarity using contextual embeddings
    \item \textbf{ROUGE-1:} Unigram overlap with reference
    \item \textbf{Key Information Retention (KIR):}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{KIR} = \frac{|\{k \in K : k \in \text{Summary}\}|}{|K|}
    \label{eq:kir}
    \end{equation}
    where $K$ is the set of extracted keyphrases
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis of Key Factors}

\textbf{Training Data Size.} Counterintuitively, increasing training data from 10,000 to 25,000 samples provides no improvement---and in several cases, performance slightly degrades. The 10k-60t configuration achieves the best results across both BERT Score and KIR, suggesting that the signature extraction task is learnable with relatively few examples. This finding has important practical implications: organizations can deploy competitive summarization systems without requiring extensive computational resources for training.

\textbf{Similarity Threshold.} The semantic similarity threshold $\theta$ shows minimal impact on downstream performance. Across all three thresholds tested (0.60, 0.65, 0.70), BERT Score varies by less than 1\%, and KIR differences remain within 3 percentage points. This robustness to hyperparameter choices is encouraging, as it suggests that practitioners need not invest significant effort in threshold tuning. The slight advantage of $\theta$=0.60 in KIR (approximately 47\% vs 46\% for higher thresholds) indicates that a more permissive threshold captures additional relevant signatures without introducing excessive noise.

\textbf{Quantization Effects.} Perhaps surprisingly, 4-bit and 8-bit quantization produce nearly identical results. The average BERT Score difference is only 0.0002 (0.6611 vs 0.6609), and KIR differs by less than 0.4 percentage points (45.40\% vs 45.05\%). This finding validates the use of aggressive quantization for deployment, enabling the full pipeline to run on consumer GPUs with approximately 8GB of VRAM without sacrificing quality. Considering this, we decide to employ the 4-bit configuration for our experiments, effectively preventing GPU \textit{Out of Memory} (OOM) errors. 

\textbf{Prompting Strategy.} An interesting trade-off emerges between zero-shot and few-shot prompting. Zero-shot achieves higher KIR (47.12\% vs 43.33\%), suggesting that examples may actually distract the model from focusing on the extracted signatures. Conversely, few-shot provides marginally higher BERT Score (0.6621 vs 0.6599), likely because the examples guide the model toward more fluent output. The optimal choice depends on the application: domains where missing critical information is costly (medical, legal) should prefer zero-shot, while applications prioritizing stylistic consistency may benefit from few-shot demonstrations.

The hyperparameter selection was tailored to prioritize factual accuracy. \subsection{Prompt engineering}
Due to the higher result obtained with zero-shot prompting, a rigorous process of prompt engineering was conducted and the results were validated over the test set. Table~\ref{tab:prompt_template} shows the latest version of the prompt, that reaches the best results.\\
Inside this prompt, three primary sections can be recognized:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Security rules} ('REGOLE DI SICUREZZA'): aim to bypass the model's safety filters regarding sensitive or medical information. The core strategy is to frame the task as a purely objective summarization exercise, devoid of any promotional or harmful intent;
    \item  \textbf{Accuracy rules} ('REGOLE CRITICHE DI FEDELTÀ'): this section is designed to mitigate hallucinations from the model. The central theme is strict adherence to the source text: any information not explicitly present in the provided source must be excluded from the generated summary;
    \item \textbf{Writing rules} ('REGOLE DI SCRITTURA'): these instructions define the stylistic requirements of the summary. Furthermore, they serve to prevent the model from simply copying and pasting segments of the original text.
\end{enumerate}
While this prompt successfully reduces hallucinations, it exhibits dual behavior concerning model refusals. Under Stochastic Decoding configurations, it effectively minimizes the occurrence of refusals; conversely, under Greedy Decoding, it tends to act as a trigger, inadvertently provoking the model to refuse the task.

\subsection{Abstraction Quality Analysis}

Traditional metrics like ROUGE and BERT Score measure similarity to reference summaries but do not capture whether the model truly \textit{abstracts} information or merely extracts and rearranges it. To address this limitation, we introduce additional evaluation dimensions that specifically target abstraction quality.

\textbf{Abstraction Metrics.} We compute three complementary measures on 100 samples using an optimized abstractive prompt: (1) \textit{Abstraction Score} = 1 minus the trigram overlap between generated summary and source, where higher values indicate less verbatim copying (observed: 0.57); (2) \textit{Novel N-grams} = percentage of bigrams in the summary not present in the source, measuring lexical novelty (observed: 43.3\%); (3) \textit{Compression Ratio} = summary length divided by source length, indicating conciseness (observed: 32.2\%). All the previous data refer to the Greedy Decoding configuration.

\textbf{LLM-as-Judge.} Following recent work on using LLMs for evaluation~\cite{goyal2022news}, we employ the same Llama-3.1-8B model with a dedicated evaluation prompt to rate generated summaries on four dimensions, each on a 1--5 scale. 
The evaluation phase was characterized by a significant refinement process of the LLM-as-a-judge framework, transitioning from a baseline to a \textbf{Source-Aware} configuration. The primary differences are listed in Table~\ref{tab:prompt_evolution}. This evolution was necessitated by the limitation of the initial prompt, which frequently exhibited limited discriminative power and generic feedback due to a strict 15-word constraint on reasoning. By implementing a \textbf{sentence-by-sentence} audit, the new prompt significantly enhanced the granularity and transparency of the assessments. However, the judge still exhibits a performance bottleneck. In particular, it is prone to hallucinations in completeness scoring, occasionally relying on its internal parametric knowledge rather than strictly adhering to the provided source. Furthermore, a dual behavior persists regarding model refusals: while the judge correctly penalizes most instances of the refusals, there were observed cases in which it erroneously assigned maximum scores to refusal-based outputs. These findings suggest that while prompt engineering successfully mitigates evaluation bias, the judge model still struggles to recognize total task refusals.

\section{Discussion}

The experimental results reveal several insights that extend beyond the immediate task of Italian summarization and speak to the broader dynamics of hybrid neuro-symbolic NLP systems.

\subsection{The Paradox of Data Efficiency}

Perhaps the most unexpected finding is that the smallest training configuration (10k samples) consistently outperforms larger ones. This contradicts the prevailing assumption that more training data yields better models. We hypothesize two explanations for this phenomenon. First, the signature extraction task may be inherently simple once semantic supervision provides clean labels---the model learns the core pattern quickly, and additional examples offer diminishing returns. Second, larger training sets may introduce label noise: even with semantic supervision, borderline cases accumulate, and the model may learn to hedge rather than commit to clear salience predictions.

This finding has significant implications for practitioners. Organizations seeking to deploy SM-SIP need not invest in massive annotation efforts; a relatively small, high-quality training set suffices. In resource-constrained settings, this represents a meaningful reduction in both computational and human annotation costs.

\subsection{Generator Dominance}

The consistently narrow performance bands across all configurations (BERT Score varies by only 0.6\%) suggest that the underlying LLM---Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct---is the primary determinant of summary quality (see Figure~\ref{fig:bert_comparison}). SigExt provides useful guidance by identifying which content to prioritize, but the generator's pre-trained linguistic knowledge and summarization capabilities ultimately shape the output.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centerline{\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{figures/comparison_bert.png}}
\caption{BERT Score across all 16 configurations. The narrow range (0.659--0.665) demonstrates that the LLM generator dominates output quality regardless of extractor configuration.}
\label{fig:bert_comparison}
\end{figure}

This observation raises an important architectural question: if the generator dominates, what is the value of the extractor? Our KIR results provide the answer. Without signature guidance, LLMs may omit critical information or hallucinate. The extractor's role is not to improve fluency (the LLM handles that) but to ensure \textit{coverage}---that key information from the source makes it into the final summary.

\subsection{Robustness and Deployment Considerations}

The equivalence of 4-bit and 8-bit quantization is particularly relevant for deployment. Modern quantization techniques like NF4 preserve model quality while reducing memory requirements by approximately 50\%. Our results confirm that this holds for the summarization pipeline, enabling deployment on consumer hardware without quality degradation.

Similarly, the robustness to threshold selection ($\theta \in \{0.60, 0.65, 0.70\}$) simplifies hyperparameter tuning. Practitioners can select any reasonable threshold without extensive cross-validation, accelerating the path from development to production.

\subsection{Zero-Shot vs Few-Shot Trade-offs}

The inverse relationship between prompting strategy and metrics deserves careful interpretation. Zero-shot prompting achieves higher KIR (47\% vs 43\%), while few-shot achieves higher BERT Score. We believe this reflects a fundamental tension: few-shot examples encourage the model to mimic the stylistic patterns of demonstrations, potentially at the cost of attending closely to the extracted signatures. Zero-shot prompting, lacking such templates, forces the model to rely more heavily on the provided keyphrases.

For applications where missing information carries high cost---such as medical or legal summarization---zero-shot is preferable. For applications prioritizing stylistic consistency or brand voice, few-shot offers better alignment with exemplars.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented SM-SIP, an extension of the Salient Information Prompting framework that introduces semantic supervision for training label generation and validates the approach on Italian, a morphologically rich language where traditional fuzzy matching fails.

Our experiments on the WITS dataset demonstrate that the approach achieves competitive performance, with BERT Score of 0.66 and Key Information Retention reaching 49\%. More importantly, the results reveal that the signature extraction task is learnable with minimal training data---the best-performing configuration uses only 10,000 samples, outperforming models trained on 25,000 examples. This finding suggests that semantic supervision provides sufficiently clean labels that the extractor converges quickly.

The practical implications are significant. The pipeline runs efficiently on consumer GPUs using 4-bit quantization without quality loss. The robustness to hyperparameter choices (threshold, quantization, prompting strategy) simplifies deployment. And the data efficiency reduces the annotation burden for organizations seeking to adopt the approach.

Looking forward, the semantic supervision methodology opens possibilities for adaptation to other languages and domains. The key insight---that training on semantic similarity rather than string matching produces better extractors---should generalize wherever paraphrasing is common in reference summaries.
