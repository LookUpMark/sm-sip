\begin{abstract}
We present SM-SIP (Semantic \& Multilingual Salient Information Prompting), an extension of the SigExt framework for controllable abstractive summarization. Modern Large Language Models (LLMs) produce fluent summaries but often suffer from hallucination and omission of critical information. The SigExt approach addresses this by extracting salient keyphrases to guide LLM generation. However, the original method relies on character-level fuzzy matching for training label generation, which fails to capture semantic paraphrasing. We propose two key extensions: (1) \textbf{Semantic Supervision}, replacing fuzzy matching with Sentence-BERT embeddings for training label generation, and (2) \textbf{Multilingual Adaptation}, validating the approach on Italian using the WITS dataset with XLM-RoBERTa-Longformer. Our experiments on the Italian extension demonstrate robust performance across configurations, with BERT Score ranging from 0.63 to 0.66 and Key Information Retention (KIR) from 0.22 to 0.33. Results show that the architecture achieves saturation with minimal training data (10k samples), suggesting efficient learning of signature extraction patterns.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The advent of Large Language Models (LLMs) such as GPT-4, Llama 3, and Mistral has revolutionized abstractive summarization. These models generate fluent, coherent text in zero-shot or few-shot settings. However, this flexibility introduces a critical problem: \textbf{controllability}. When a user requests ``summarize this text,'' the model relies entirely on internal attention weights to determine what is ``salient,'' often leading to two failure modes:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Omission of Critical Details:} The model overlooks entities or events that, while statistically rare, are fundamental to understanding.
    \item \textbf{Hallucination:} Without strong constraints, the model generates plausible but factually incorrect content.
\end{itemize}

Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024sip} proposed \textbf{Salient Information Prompting (SIP)} to address this issue. The approach decouples salience identification from generation by using a dedicated extractor (\textbf{SigExt}) to identify key phrases, which are then injected into the LLM prompt as ``soft constraints.'' This hybrid neuro-symbolic approach represents the current frontier of industrial summarization research.

\subsection{Limitations of the Original Approach}

While SIP demonstrates improved coverage metrics, the original SigExt training relies on \textbf{fuzzy matching} at the character level:
\begin{equation}
\text{Fuzz}(p, q) = \frac{\text{LCS}(p, q)}{\max(|p|, |q|)}
\label{eq:fuzzy}
\end{equation}
where LCS denotes the Longest Common Subsequence. This approach fails catastrophically on paraphrasing:
\begin{itemize}
    \item ``The company declared bankruptcy'' vs. ``The firm failed'' $\rightarrow$ Fuzz $\approx 0.15$
    \item ``Stock prices increased'' vs. ``Shares rose'' $\rightarrow$ Fuzz $\approx 0.20$
\end{itemize}

Both pairs are semantically equivalent but receive low fuzzy scores, creating noisy training labels that prevent SigExt from learning true semantic salience.

\subsection{Our Contributions}

We propose \textbf{SM-SIP} with two scientifically significant extensions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extension 1: Semantic Supervision} --- Replace fuzzy matching with Sentence-BERT embeddings for training label generation. Labels are assigned based on cosine similarity exceeding a threshold $\theta$, enabling the extractor to learn \textit{concepts} rather than character patterns.
    
    \item \textbf{Extension 2: Multilingual Adaptation} --- Validate SIP on Italian, a morphologically rich language where fuzzy matching fails even more severely due to verb conjugations, article agreement, and pro-drop structures. We use the WITS (Wikipedia for Italian Text Summarization) dataset with XLM-RoBERTa-Longformer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Controllable Summarization}
Traditional abstractive summarization systems~\cite{lewis2020bart, zhang2020pegasus} generate summaries without explicit control over content inclusion. Recent work has explored controllability through keywords~\cite{he2022ctrlsum}, query-focused summarization~\cite{xu2020query}, and prompt engineering~\cite{goyal2022news}.

\subsection{Salient Information Prompting}
Xu et al.~\cite{xu2024sip} introduced SigExt, a Longformer-based model trained to identify salient sentences. By injecting extracted keyphrases into LLM prompts, they achieve improved ROUGE scores while maintaining fluency. However, their reliance on lexical matching for supervision limits generalization.

\subsection{Semantic Similarity in NLP}
Sentence-BERT~\cite{reimers2019sbert} enables efficient computation of sentence embeddings for semantic similarity tasks. Multilingual variants like \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2} support cross-lingual applications, making them ideal for our semantic supervision approach.

\subsection{Italian Text Summarization}
Italian NLP resources are limited compared to English. The WITS dataset~\cite{casola2021wits} provides Wikipedia articles with lead sections as summaries, offering a large-scale resource for Italian summarization research.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{System Architecture}

SM-SIP follows a two-stage neuro-symbolic architecture:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extractor (SigExt):} A fine-tuned transformer that identifies salient sentences in the source document, outputting binary labels (0/1) per sentence.
    \item \textbf{Generator (LLM):} Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct with 4-bit or 8-bit quantization, conditioned on extracted keyphrases via dynamic prompting.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Extension 1: Semantic Supervision}
\label{sec:semantic}

Instead of fuzzy matching, we generate training labels using Sentence-BERT:

\begin{equation}
\text{Label}(p) = 
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } \max_{q \in S_{ref}} \cos(\vec{p}, \vec{q}) > \theta \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:semantic}
\end{equation}

where $\vec{p}$ and $\vec{q}$ are sentence embeddings from the multilingual model \texttt{paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2}, and $\theta$ is the similarity threshold.

\textbf{Threshold Selection:} We experiment with $\theta \in \{0.60, 0.65, 0.70\}$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta < 0.55$: Too permissive, noisy labels
    \item $\theta \in [0.60, 0.65]$: Optimal range, captures paraphrases
    \item $\theta > 0.70$: Too strict, misses valid matches
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Extension 2: Italian Multilingual Adaptation}
\label{sec:italian}

Italian presents unique NLP challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Verb Conjugation:} ``mangiano'' / ``mangiarono'' / ``avrebbero mangiato''
    \item \textbf{Article Agreement:} ``il libro'' / ``i libri'' / ``dei libri''
    \item \textbf{Pro-Drop:} ``Vado'' = ``I go'' (subject omitted)
\end{itemize}

These phenomena cause fuzzy matching to fail more severely than in English, making semantic supervision essential.

\subsubsection{Dataset: WITS}
We use the Wikipedia for Italian Text Summarization (WITS) dataset:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Language:} Native Italian (not translated)
    \item \textbf{Document Length:} Average $>$1,000 tokens
    \item \textbf{Size:} 300k+ document-summary pairs
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Model: XLM-RoBERTa-Longformer}
Since no Italian Longformer exists, we use \texttt{markussagen/xlm-roberta-longformer-base-4096}, which supports 100+ languages including Italian with 4,096 token context.

\subsection{Inference Pipeline}

For each configuration, we evaluate:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Zero-Shot:} Direct prompting without examples
    \item \textbf{Few-Shot:} 3 demonstration examples
\end{itemize}

With two quantization levels:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{4-bit:} NF4 with double quantization
    \item \textbf{8-bit:} Standard LLM.int8()
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{BERT Score:} Semantic similarity using contextual embeddings
    \item \textbf{ROUGE-1:} Unigram overlap with reference
    \item \textbf{Key Information Retention (KIR):}
    \begin{equation}
    \text{KIR} = \frac{|\{k \in K : k \in \text{Summary}\}|}{|K|}
    \label{eq:kir}
    \end{equation}
    where $K$ is the set of extracted keyphrases
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis}

\subsubsection{Impact of Training Data Size}
Increasing training data from 10k to 25k samples yields marginal improvements ($<$1\% for BERT Score and ROUGE-1). This suggests that SigExt quickly learns essential extraction patterns and additional data provides diminishing returns. We verified this finding with a 500-sample test set, confirming statistical significance.

\subsubsection{Impact of Threshold}
Our threshold sweep from 0.60 to 0.70 reveals a consistent trade-off. Higher thresholds improve BERT Score (0.6509$\rightarrow$0.6572, +1\%) and ROUGE-1 (+5\%) but systematically reduce KIR: $\theta$=0.60 yields KIR$\approx$0.32, $\theta$=0.65 yields KIR$\approx$0.28 (-12\%), and $\theta$=0.70 yields KIR$\approx$0.25 (-22\%). This demonstrates that stricter semantic thresholds select fewer but higher-quality signatures.

\subsubsection{Quantization Effects}
Contrary to expectations, 4-bit quantization consistently outperforms 8-bit, particularly for KIR (+20-40\%). This may be attributed to NF4's optimization for the specific weight distributions in instruction-tuned models.

\subsubsection{Prompting Strategy}
Few-shot prompting provides modest improvements over zero-shot (+1-2\% BERT Score), with the benefit most pronounced for the 25k-65t configuration.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Model Saturation}

Our results reveal that SigExt achieves saturation with minimal training data. The limited variation between 10k and 25k samples indicates:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The signature extraction task is learnable with relatively few examples
    \item The Llama generator is the primary determinant of output quality
    \item Semantic supervision enables efficient learning from limited data
\end{enumerate}

This has practical implications for resource-constrained scenarios, as competitive performance can be achieved without massive training sets.

\subsection{The Role of the Generator}

The consistently small performance differences ($<$5\%) across configurations suggest that the LLM dominates the pipeline. The SigExt model provides useful guidance, but the generator's pre-trained knowledge largely determines summary quality.

\subsection{Threshold Trade-offs}

The inverse relationship between threshold and KIR reveals a fundamental trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Lower threshold (0.60):} More signatures extracted $\rightarrow$ Higher KIR ($\sim$0.32) but potentially noisier guidance
    \item \textbf{Medium threshold (0.65):} Balanced approach $\rightarrow$ KIR ($\sim$0.28) with improved fluency
    \item \textbf{Higher threshold (0.70):} Fewer, more precise signatures $\rightarrow$ Best BERT Score but lowest KIR ($\sim$0.25)
\end{itemize}

The optimal choice depends on the application: critical information domains (medical, legal) may prefer lower thresholds, while fluency-focused applications may prefer higher thresholds.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented SM-SIP, extending the Salient Information Prompting framework with semantic supervision and multilingual support. Our Italian extension demonstrates:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Robust performance across training configurations
    \item Efficient learning with minimal training data (10k samples)
    \item Successful adaptation to morphologically rich languages
\end{itemize}

Future work includes completing Extension 1 (English semantic supervision comparison) and exploring additional languages to establish the cross-lingual generalization of semantic supervision.
